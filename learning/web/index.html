Learning Beats Digging: Why Clean Energy Keeps Getting Cheaper

Solar panels and batteries are made in factories, and they get cheaper the more of them the world makes. Oil has to be dug up and burned, over and over, and it doesn't. See how much each doubling takes off the price, follow it a few doublings ahead, and see how long that takes.

About this model

This is a very simple model of learning by doing: the more of something the world makes, the cheaper each one gets. It goes with section 12.5 of Chapter 12 of the book, "Learning beats digging", and it draws the same data as the chapter's Figures 12.4 and 12.16.

The rule it uses is a learning curve. Each time the world doubles all it has ever made of something, the price falls by about the same share. That share is the learning rate. For solar panels it has been 24.3% for each doubling since 1975. For lithium-ion batteries it has been 19.5% since 1991. Oil has no learning rate: each doubling of all the oil ever produced has raised its price by about 10.0%, after inflation.

On the page as it opens, solar panels cost $0.120 a watt in 2025. Three more doublings at the chapter's "about 20%" would bring them to $0.0616 a watt. At the speed of the last five years, those three doublings take about 8.6 years.

How to use this page

The page has two views. "Learning curves" plots price against all of it ever made, on logarithmic axes, as Figure 12.16 does. Choose solar panels, batteries or oil. Each year is a point, and a dashed straight line runs through them. From the last point, two lines run on: one at the technology's own past rate, and one at the rate you choose.

You can change two things: the price fall with each doubling, from −20% to 40%, and the doublings still to come, from 1 to 6. The page opens on solar panels, at 20% and 3 doublings. "Use its own past rate" sets the slider to the rate the points give. "Back to where the page opened" puts everything back.

"Against coal and gas" is Figure 12.4: the cost of electricity from new solar and wind farms around the world, 2010 to 2025, against IRENA's range for new coal and gas plants.

The address in your browser changes as you move the sliders, so you can bookmark a view or send it to someone. Nothing is sent anywhere as you work.

This page is part of the website for Climate Change: Simple, Serious, Solvable, an open textbook by Scott Denning. It goes with Chapter 12, "Reinventing Fire", section 12.5.

The equations

You don't need any of this to use the page. But the whole model is six short steps, and you can follow every one of them with a calculator. The ln key on a calculator is the natural logarithm.

The model answers one question: how much does the price fall each time the world doubles all it has ever made, and what happens if that goes on?

For each year the data give two numbers: X, all of it ever made up to that year, and P, its price that year, after inflation. X0 is the first year's X.

1. How many times has it doubled?

One doubling multiplies X by 2, two multiply it by 4, and n multiply it by 2n. Turned around, the number of doublings from X0 to X is

n=ln⁡X/X0ln⁡2

That's why the graph's axes are logarithmic: each doubling is the same distance along the bottom.

2. The straight line through the points

On a graph with logarithmic axes, a learning curve is a straight line:

ln⁡P=a+b⁢ln⁡X

The model finds the a and b that bring the line closest to every year's point (a least-squares fit, the method Figure 12.16 uses). b is the line's slope. For solar panels it's −0.402; for oil it's +0.138, a line that rises.

3. The learning rate

Double X, and step 2 adds b ln 2 to ln P, which multiplies the price by 2b. So each doubling takes off a share

L=1−2b

of the price. That's the learning rate. When b is above zero, L is below zero, and the price rises with each doubling.

4. The price after more doublings

Each doubling multiplies the price by 1 − L. After N more of them, starting from the last year's price P, it's

PN=P⁢(1−L)N

The page works this out twice: once with the technology's own L from step 3, and once with the L you choose.

5. How fast is it growing?

The model takes today's speed as the average over the last five years. If all of it ever made was Xearlier five years before the last year, it grew each year by

g=(X/Xearlier)15−1

as a share: 0.273 is 27.3% a year.

6. How long do the doublings take?

Growing by g a year, X doubles in ln 2 ÷ ln(1 + g) years. So N doublings take

T=N⁢ln⁡2ln⁡(1+g)

years. This is the rule of 70 that bankers use for money: at 7% a year, money doubles in about 10 years.

Where the equations stop

  • A curve isn't a law. A learning curve describes the past. Nothing guarantees the next doublings will cut the price as the last ones did. Prices have gone up for a year or more before: solar panels from 2005 to 2007 and in 2022, and batteries in 2022. The points on the graph show it.
  • Doublings get harder. Each one means building as much again as in all of history. At some point growth has to slow, and the doublings come further apart.
  • Panels aren't power plants. The solar curve is the price of panels. A solar farm also needs land, steel, wiring and labor, which learn more slowly, so the cost of its electricity falls more slowly than its panels' price.
  • Different dollars. Solar and oil are in 2025 dollars, and batteries in 2024 dollars, as in Figure 12.16. The learning rate doesn't depend on the dollar year.
  • Oil isn't the whole story. The book shows oil because its data are open. Coal and gas don't learn either, and the "Against coal and gas" view compares their power plants with solar and wind.

A worked example

Open the page and leave everything where it is: solar panels, 20% and 3 doublings. Here's where the table's numbers come from.

  1. Doublings so far (step 1). The world had installed 0.00054 GW of solar by 1975 and 2,378 GW by 2025. n = ln(2,378 ÷ 0.00054) ÷ ln 2 = 15.30 ÷ 0.693 = 22.1 doublings.
  2. The line (step 2). The fit gives b = −0.402.
  3. Solar's own learning rate (step 3). L = 1 − 2−0.402 = 1 − 0.757 = 0.243, or 24.3% for each doubling.
  4. Three more doublings at 20% (step 4). PN = 0.12022 × (1 − 0.20)3 = 0.12022 × 0.512 = $0.0616 a watt.
  5. Today's speed (step 5). The world had installed 711 GW by 2020. g = (2,378 ÷ 711)1/5 − 1 = 0.273, or 27.3% a year.
  6. How long (step 6). T = 3 × 0.693 ÷ ln 1.273 = 2.079 ÷ 0.241 = 8.6 years.

The table says 22.1 doublings, 24.3% for each, 27.3% a year, $0.0616 a watt at 20% and 8.6 years.

See the equations at work

  1. Solar at its own rate. Press "Use its own past rate". Three more doublings at 24.3% bring panels to $0.0521 a watt, and the world would have installed about 19,000 GW of solar by then (step 4).
  2. Batteries. Choose batteries. Their own rate is 19.5%, the chapter's "about 20%". All the batteries ever made grew 34.8% a year over the last five years, so each doubling takes about 2.3 years (steps 5 and 6).
  3. Oil. Choose oil and press "Use its own past rate". The rate is −10.0%: each doubling has raised the price. And all the oil ever produced grows only 2.1% a year now, so one doubling takes about 34 years (step 6).

How it was checked

A set of tests comes with the model, and they run every time the page changes. Here's what they hold it to.

  • The data, against the book's figures. The page's data file is made from the files that draw Figures 12.4 and 12.16, and each is checked against the fingerprint (a SHA-256) the figure package records. The fit must give the figure's learning rates: 24.3% for solar panels, 19.5% for batteries and about +10% for oil.
  • The code, against a second copy. The tests have the six steps written out a second time, in another programming language. At every setting they try, the two copies agree to within a billionth.
  • This tab, against the model. Every equation above is tried with the model's own numbers. The worked example is done again from the rounded numbers a calculator would use. And each number in the text is worked out again from the model, and the chapter's words are looked up in Chapter 12.

The code

The code is small enough to read. Two files do all the work, and they use no outside software. The comments in model.js use the same step numbers and letters as the equations above.
