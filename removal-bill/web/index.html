The Mop and the Bill: What It Costs to Take Carbon Back Out of the Air

Burning carbon is a running faucet, and taking CO₂ back out of the air is mopping the floor. Choose how much carbon to mop up each year, for how long and at what price. See the bill in dollars and in energy, and how much cooler it makes the planet.

Open in a new window

A GtC is a billion tons of carbon. In 2025 the world emitted about 11.5 GtC and captured about 0.0005. The IPCC's scenarios that hold warming to 2 °C count on about 1.6 a year by 2050.

Chapter 13's prices: often $50 to $200 for BECCS, $1,000 or more for direct air capture today, and $230 to $540 projected for a large industry.

Direct air capture takes about 2,630 to 4,380 kWh a ton with today's solvent design, most of it heat, and about 1,390 if hopes for efficiency come true. BECCS makes energy instead of using it: set this to 0.

      About this model

      This is a very simple model of carbon dioxide removal: capturing CO₂ from the air and storing it where it can't get back. It does the arithmetic of Chapter 13 of the book, "Cleaning Up the Mess": what removal would cost, in dollars and in energy, and how much good it would do.

      Three ideas from the book are built in. One part per million (ppm) of CO₂ in the air weighs about 7.81 billion tons. About half of whatever is taken out of the air comes back out of the land and ocean. And the planet cools by about 2 °C for every 1,000 GtC taken out and kept out (a GtC is a billion tons of carbon). That's the same rule that says how much it warms when the carbon goes in.

      On the page as it opens, the world captures 1 GtC a year for 50 years, at $100 for each ton of CO₂. That's 50 GtC, or 183 billion tons of CO₂, and the bill is $18.3 trillion. The CO₂ in the air falls by 11.7 ppm, and the planet cools by 0.10 °C (0.18 °F). Chapter 13 calls that the price of a tenth of a degree.

      How to use this page

      You can change four things: how much carbon is captured and stored each year, from 0 to 12 GtC; for how many years, from 1 to 100; the price of capturing and storing each ton of CO₂, from $10 to $1,000; and the energy each ton takes, from 0 to 5,000 kilowatt-hours (kWh). The page opens at 1 GtC a year for 50 years, at $100 and 1,390 kWh a ton.

      Two buttons set the price and the energy together. "Chapter 13's hopeful numbers" sets $100 and 1,390 kWh a ton. "Direct air capture today" sets $1,000 and 4,380 kWh a ton. "Back to where the page opened" puts all four back.

      The graph shows the carbon as the years go by. The dark line is all the carbon captured and stored. The green line, half as steep, is how far the carbon in the air has fallen. The band between them is what came back out of the land and ocean.

      Under the graph, the first set of bars holds your bill up against things you know: what the world spends on phones and phone service in a year, what it would cost to build every paved road on Earth, and one estimate of a program to dim the Sun. The second set holds the energy your capture uses each year against all the electricity the world made in 2025. Then a table gives every number, and two prices that depend only on your price for a ton: what it costs to lower the CO₂ in the air by 1 ppm, and what it costs to cool the planet by a tenth of a degree.

      The address in your browser changes as you move the sliders, so you can bookmark a view or send it to someone. Nothing is sent anywhere as you work.

      This page is part of the website for Climate Change: Simple, Serious, Solvable, an open textbook by Scott Denning. It goes with Chapter 13, "Cleaning Up the Mess", and its Table 13.1.

      The equations

      You don't need any of this to use the page. But the whole model is six short sums, and you can follow every one of them with a calculator.

      The model answers one question: if the world captures carbon from the air and stores it, what does that cost, and how much does it cool the planet?

      You choose four things: R, the carbon captured and stored, in GtC a year; N, the number of years; p, the price, in dollars for each ton of CO₂; and e, the energy, in kWh for each ton of CO₂. The rest is fixed:

      1. How much carbon is captured?

      Capture R GtC every year for N years, and in all you've captured

      C=R⁢N

      GtC. Picture Chapter 9's pile of coal on the National Mall: each GtC is one of those piles, put back.

      2. How much CO₂ is that?

      The carbon in the air isn't coal. Burning hooked each carbon atom to two oxygen atoms. A carbon atom weighs 12 and an oxygen atom 16, so a molecule of CO₂ weighs 12 + 16 + 16 = 44. Each ton of carbon is 44/12 = 3.67 tons of CO₂, and it's the CO₂ that has to be caught and stored:

      M=4412⁢C

      billion tons of CO₂. Prices for removal are quoted for a ton of CO₂, not a ton of carbon, so the bill needs M.

      3. What does it cost?

      Every ton costs the same price, p. So the bill is

      B=p⁢M

      billion dollars: dollars a ton, times billions of tons. A thousand billion is a trillion.

      4. How much energy does it take?

      Each year the capture handles 44/12 × R billion tons of CO₂, and each ton takes e kilowatt-hours. So each year it uses

      W=e⁢4412⁢R

      terawatt-hours (TWh). A terawatt-hour is a billion kilowatt-hours: one kilowatt-hour for each of a billion tons. The page compares W with all the electricity the world made in 2025, about 31,800 TWh. That's a comparison of size only: most of the energy direct air capture needs is heat, not electricity.

      5. How far does the CO₂ in the air fall?

      First, what does one ppm weigh? One ppm is a millionth of the air's molecules. All the air weighs 5.15 × 1018 kg, and a molecule of CO₂ weighs 44 where an average molecule of air weighs 29. So one ppm of CO₂ weighs 1/1,000,000 × 5.15 × 1018 kg × 44/29 = 7.81 × 1012 kg. That's m = 7.81 billion tons of CO₂, or 2.13 GtC.

      Now the half that comes back. The land and ocean took up about half of what we emitted, and Chapter 9 explains why they give carbon back when the CO₂ in the air falls. So lowering the air's CO₂ by one ton takes about two tons captured, and the CO₂ in the air falls by

      x=M2⁢m

      ppm. The 2 under the line is the half that comes back. Without it, the same capture would lower the CO₂ twice as far.

      6. How much cooler does it get?

      Chapter 9's rule of thumb is about 2 °C of warming for every 1,000 GtC emitted. Taking carbon out undoes about what putting it in did, so the cooling is

      T=21,000⁢C

      degrees Celsius. Don't halve this one. The rule was measured with the land and ocean doing their part, so it already counts the half that comes back. It uses C, all the carbon captured.

      The two prices in the table

      Turn steps 3 and 5 around, and you get the price of 1 ppm. Lowering the CO₂ in the air by 1 ppm takes 2 × 7.81 = 15.6 billion tons captured. At $100 a ton, that's $1.56 trillion. Lowering it by 100 ppm would cost $156 trillion.

      Turn steps 2, 3 and 6 around, and you get the price of a tenth of a degree. A tenth of a degree takes 0.1 ÷ 0.002 = 50 GtC, which is 183 billion tons of CO₂. At $100 a ton, that's $18.3 trillion. Both prices go up and down in step with p, and neither depends on how fast the carbon is captured.

      Where the equations stop

      • The mop alone. The page shows what removal does, and leaves out the faucet. In 2025 the world emitted about 11.5 GtC, 10.4 of it from fossil fuels. Unless those emissions stop, the CO₂ in the air keeps rising, only more slowly, and the planet keeps warming.
      • Round numbers. Half is a round number. So is 2 °C for every 1,000 GtC, the book's teaching value. With the IPCC's best estimate, about 1.65 °C for every 1,000 GtC, a tenth of a degree takes about 60 GtC, not 50.
      • One price for every ton. The model charges the first ton and the last ton the same. It doesn't ask whether there's enough land, rock, energy or storage for the amount you choose. Table 13.1 in the book says what 1 GtC a year would take for each method.
      • Every ton stays stored. The model counts each ton once and assumes it never leaks. A forest that burns gives its carbon back.
      • No other gases. The cooling is from CO₂ alone.

      A worked example

      Open the page and leave everything where it is: 1 GtC a year for 50 years, at $100 and 1,390 kWh a ton. Here's where the table's numbers come from.

      1. The carbon captured (step 1). C = 1 × 50 = 50 GtC.
      2. The CO₂ (step 2). M = 44 ÷ 12 × 50 = 183.3 billion tons of CO₂.
      3. The bill (step 3). B = 100 × 183.3 = 18,330 billion dollars, which is $18.3 trillion.
      4. The energy each year (step 4). W = 1,390 × 44 ÷ 12 × 1 = 5,097 TWh a year. And 5,097 ÷ 31,800 = 0.16, so that's as much energy as 16% of the world's electricity.
      5. How far the CO₂ falls (step 5). x = 183.3 ÷ (2 × 7.81) = 183.3 ÷ 15.62 = 11.7 ppm.
      6. The cooling (step 6). T = 2 ÷ 1,000 × 50 = 0.10 °C.

      The table says 50 GtC (183 billion tons of CO₂), a bill of $18.3 trillion, 5,097 TWh a year, 11.7 ppm and 0.10 °C.

      A few details

      • Tons. A ton here is a metric ton, 1,000 kg. A billion is a thousand million, and a trillion is a thousand billion.
      • Kilowatt-hours. Chapter 13 gives direct air capture's energy as 300 to 500 megawatts, around the clock, for each million tons of CO₂ a year. A year has 8,760 hours, so 500 megawatts is 4,380 kWh for each ton, and 300 is 2,630. The hopeful 1,390 kWh is 5 billion joules a ton: about half the energy that burning coal gave us when it made that ton of CO₂.
      • Dollars. The prices are for capturing and storing a ton, as the studies quote them. Nothing is discounted or adjusted for inflation: the bill is the price times the tons.

      See the equations at work

      1. One ppm in a year. Set the carbon to 4.3 GtC a year and the years to 1. The CO₂ in the air falls by 1.0 ppm, the bill is $1.58 trillion, and the capture uses 21,916 TWh: as much energy as 69% of the world's electricity (steps 3, 4 and 5). Chapter 13 says about two-thirds.
      2. Today's machines. Press "Back to where the page opened", then "Direct air capture today". The price is ten times higher, so the bill for a tenth of a degree is $183 trillion. Each GtC a year uses 16,060 TWh: as much energy as 51% of the world's electricity (step 4).
      3. Mopping as fast as the faucet runs. Press "Back to where the page opened", then set the carbon to 10.4 GtC a year, what the world emitted from fossil fuels in 2025. Even at the hopeful 1,390 kWh a ton, the capture uses 53,005 TWh a year: as much energy as 1.7 times all the world's electricity (step 4).
      4. A century of mopping. Press "Back to where the page opened", then set the years to 100. The world captures 100 GtC and pays $36.7 trillion, and the planet cools by 0.20 °C (step 6).

      Where the numbers come from

      Every number in the model is Chapter 13's, and the chapter gives its sources. The sum for one ppm is from the chapter's section on carbon arithmetic, which Scott Denning credits to Morey Wolfson. These are the sources behind the numbers this page leans on most.

      The weight of all the air (the chapter rounds 5.148 to 5.15):

      Trenberth, K. E., and L. Smith (2005). The mass of the atmosphere: A constraint on global analyses. Journal of Climate, 18, 864–875. doi:10.1175/JCLI-3299.1

      The 2 °C for every 1,000 GtC is the book's teaching value for TCRE, a round number inside the range the IPCC assessed:

      IPCC (2021). Summary for Policymakers. In Climate Change 2021: The Physical Science Basis. Contribution of Working Group I to the Sixth Assessment Report of the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change. Cambridge University Press. doi:10.1017/9781009157896.001

      The energy that direct air capture takes, 300 to 500 megawatts for each million tons of CO₂ a year:

      National Academies of Sciences, Engineering, and Medicine (2019). Negative Emissions Technologies and Reliable Sequestration: A Research Agenda. The National Academies Press. doi:10.17226/25259

      What direct air capture costs today, and the $230 to $540 a ton projected for a large industry:

      Sievert, K., T. S. Schmidt, and B. Steffen (2024). Considering technology characteristics to project future costs of direct air capture. Joule, 8, 979–999. doi:10.1016/j.joule.2024.02.005

      All the electricity the world made in 2025, 31,779 TWh, which the chapter rounds to 31,800:

      Ember (2026). Global Electricity Review 2026. ember-energy.org/latest-insights/global-electricity-review-2026

      The world's emissions in 2025, 10.4 GtC from fossil fuels and 1.1 from land use:

      Friedlingstein, P., et al. (2026). Global Carbon Budget 2025. Earth System Science Data, 18, 3211–3288. doi:10.5194/essd-18-3211-2026

      The bar for dimming the Sun is from a 2018 study of a program of purpose-built aircraft that would carry sulfur into the stratosphere. It put the cost at about $2.25 billion a year, on average, over the program's first 15 years. The bar shows 15 × 2.25 = $33.8 billion. That isn't the price of the same cooling as your capture: the study priced a program, not a tenth of a degree. And it buys something different. Dimming the Sun leaves the CO₂ in the air, so the ocean keeps turning more acidic, and the particles fall out within a year or two, so the flights have to go on for as long as the CO₂ stays. Chapter 13 calls it a band-aid.

      Smith, W., and G. Wagner (2018). Stratospheric aerosol injection tactics and costs in the first 15 years of deployment. Environmental Research Letters, 13, 124001. doi:10.1088/1748-9326/aae98d

      The two other things the bill is held up against are from Chapter 1 of the book: the world spends about $1.7 trillion a year on phones and phone service, and building all the world's paved roads at 2025 prices would cost something like $35 to $70 trillion.

      How it was checked

      A set of tests comes with the model, and they run every time the page changes. Here's what they hold it to.

      • The code, against a second copy. The tests have the six sums written out a second time, in another programming language. At every setting they try, the two copies agree to within a billionth.
      • The model, against the book. The model's numbers are looked up in the chapters' own sentences. And the model has to give Chapter 13's answers: about $1.6 trillion for 1 ppm, about $18 trillion for a tenth of a degree, about $160 trillion for 100 ppm, and the chapter's four comparisons with the world's electricity: a sixth, a half, two-thirds and 1.7 times.
      • This tab, against the model. Every equation above is tried with the model's own numbers. The worked example is done again from the rounded numbers a calculator would use. And each number in the text is worked out again from the model.

      The code

      The code is small enough to read. Two files do all the work, and they use no outside software. The comments in model.js use the same step numbers and letters as the equations above.

      model.js is the science: the six steps, and the numbers from the book.
